## One page is enough {#one-page}

For an internship or OJT, one page is the right length. The person reading it usually has a pile of them, so make the first half of the page do the work: your field, your strongest skills, and one or two things you built or did.

> No job experience yet? That's normal for an intern. School projects, org work, and things you made on your own all count.

## The order that works {#order}

1. **Your name and how to reach you.** A personal email you check and your mobile number. Your city is enough. Leave out your full home address.
2. **A one-line summary.** What you study and what kind of internship you want. For example: "BS Computer Engineering student looking for an IoT or embedded systems OJT."
3. **Education.** School, course, and expected graduation. Add your required OJT hours if you know them.
4. **Skills.** Group them: tools, programming languages, or software. List only what you could talk about in an interview.
5. **Projects.** Two or three, each with what you made and what you did yourself.
6. **Experience and activities.** Part-time work, org roles, volunteering, competitions.
7. **Certificates and awards,** if you have any.

## Projects count as experience {#projects}

Write each project like a small job. Give it a name, one line on what it does, then two or three bullets on your part.

- Say what **you** did, not the whole group. "Wrote the sensor code" is clearer than "We built a device."
- Name the tools: Arduino, Figma, Excel, React, AutoCAD.
- Add a link if there's something to see: a GitHub repo, a portfolio, a short video.

## Start bullets with an action {#action-verbs}

Begin each bullet with a verb, so the reader sees what you did right away.

- Built, designed, wrote, tested, fixed, set up
- Organized, led, coordinated, trained
- Analyzed, measured, cleaned, reported
- Drew, edited, filmed, posted

"Responsible for the website" says little. "Built the org's event page in HTML and CSS" says a lot.

## Use the numbers you really have {#numbers}

Numbers make a bullet easy to picture: how many people, how many items, how long. Only use numbers that are true and that you could explain. "Logged sensor data from 4 rooms for 2 weeks" is good. A made-up percentage is worse than no number at all.

## Make it readable by application systems {#ats}

Some companies run resumes through software before a person sees them. To be safe:

- Use a simple one-column layout. Skip tables, text boxes, and icons for important details.
- Use normal section names like "Education", "Skills", and "Projects".
- Save it as a PDF, and name the file clearly: **Lastname-Firstname-Resume.pdf**.
- Use a common font at a readable size.

## Tailor it to each listing {#tailor}

You don't need a new resume for every application, but spend five minutes on it.

- Read the listing's skills. If you have one of them, make sure it's on your resume in the same words.
- Move your most relevant project to the top.
- Adjust your one-line summary to match the role.

Your skills fit on each InternQuest listing shows which skills the company asked for. That's your list to check against.

## Quick prep by field {#by-field}

### IoT & Embedded {#iot}

Put your hardware projects first. Name the boards, sensors, and languages (C, Python, Arduino). A photo or short video link of a working build helps a lot.

### Web & Mobile Dev {#web}

Link to your GitHub and one live project. Say which framework you used and what part you built: the frontend, an API, or the database.

### Data & Analytics {#data}

Show one project where you cleaned data and answered a question with it. Name the tools: Excel, SQL, Python, or a dashboard tool. Say what you found, in one line.

### Design & Multimedia {#design}

Your portfolio matters more than your resume here. Link it near the top. Pick three to five of your best pieces and explain your part in each.

### Business & Marketing {#business}

Org work and events are strong here. Mention anything you planned, promoted, or sold, and the result in plain words.

### Accounting & Finance {#accounting}

List the software you know (Excel, QuickBooks, or others) and any bookkeeping you've done, even for an org or a family business. Accuracy and attention to detail are what they look for.

### HR & Admin {#hr}

Show that you're organized and good with people: org roles, event registration, keeping records. Mention any office software you're comfortable with.

### Hospitality & Tourism {#hospitality}

Customer service experience of any kind counts, including part-time work, family businesses, and school events. Mention the languages you speak.

### Engineering (Civil, Mech, EE, ECE) {#engineering}

Name your design tools (AutoCAD and others) and any lab or design projects. If you've joined site visits or plant tours, include them.

### Communication & Media {#comms}

Link to samples: articles, captions, videos, or social posts you made. Say which platforms you've worked with.

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Personal details you don't need to share.** Your birthday, religion, civil status, and ID numbers aren't needed for an internship. Leave them out unless the company asks.
- **A photo** isn't required. Add one only if the listing asks for it.
- **Typos.** Read it out loud once, or ask a friend to check.
- **An email address that isn't professional.** A simple name-based email works best.
- **Skills you can't talk about.** If it's on the page, expect a question about it.
